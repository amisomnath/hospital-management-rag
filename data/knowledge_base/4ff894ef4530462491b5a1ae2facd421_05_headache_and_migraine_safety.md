# Headache and Migraine Safety


> **Educational use only:** This file provides general health information. It is not a diagnosis, prescription, or substitute for care from a qualified clinician. In a medical emergency, contact local emergency services immediately.


## Common headache patterns

Headache may occur with dehydration, lack of sleep, stress, eye strain, skipped meals, viral illness, medication overuse, migraine, or many other causes. A chatbot cannot reliably determine the cause from symptoms alone.

## Migraine features may include

- Moderate or severe headache, often throbbing.
- Pain that may affect one side or both sides.
- Nausea or vomiting.
- Sensitivity to light, sound, or smells.
- Worsening with normal activity.
- An aura in some people, such as visual changes or tingling.

A first or unusual neurological symptom should not automatically be assumed to be migraine.

## General self-care for a familiar mild headache

- Rest in a quiet environment.
- Drink fluids.
- Eat if a meal was missed.
- Reduce bright light and screen strain.
- Use only medicines that are safe for the individual and follow the package or clinician's instructions.
- Keep a headache diary when headaches recur.

## Seek medical review when

- Headaches are new, frequent, worsening, or disrupting daily life.
- Pain medicine is needed repeatedly.
- Headache starts after a new medicine.
- Headache occurs during pregnancy or soon after delivery.
- There are visual symptoms, jaw pain, scalp tenderness, unexplained weight loss, or fever.
- The person has cancer, immune suppression, or a clotting disorder.

## Emergency warning signs

- A sudden, explosive, or “worst-ever” headache.
- Headache after a significant head injury.
- Headache with weakness, facial droop, speech difficulty, severe confusion, seizure, or loss of consciousness.
- Headache with stiff neck, fever, light sensitivity, or a non-blanching rash.
- Headache with sudden loss of vision.
- Severe headache during pregnancy or after childbirth, especially with high blood pressure, swelling, or visual changes.

## Medication safety

Frequent use of pain relievers can itself contribute to recurring headaches. A clinician should review persistent or frequently treated headaches. Aspirin is not suitable for many children and teenagers and should not be given unless specifically advised by a clinician.
